Why is CD audio 44.1 kHz?

Human hearing reaches about 20 kHz. The theorem needs a rate above 2 × 20 kHz, so over 40 kHz. CD audio uses 44.1 kHz, which leaves extra width for a low-pass filter. The exact value was chosen to suit the recording equipment of that era.

How to read this

Show how to read this
  • Sampling theorem: if the highest frequency in a wave is fmax, you can rebuild the wave exactly when the sampling rate fs is greater than 2 × fmax (fs > 2 × fmax). In the chart above, f plays the role of fmax.
  • Aliasing: for example, sample a 9 Hz wave at 10 Hz and it looks like a slow 1 Hz wave. When fs is too low, a fast wave turns into a slower one like this. The apparent frequency is the size of the difference between f and the multiple of fs closest to f (example: the gap between 9 and 10 is 1 Hz). In a DAW, you hear this as aliasing: inharmonic, metallic-sounding tones.
  • Exactly 2× is the borderline. The samples can land where the wave looks flat, so this chart shifts the sample points slightly (phase 0.3, a small offset in where the wave starts) to keep the wave visible.

Practice questions (original)

Question 1 A sound contains frequencies up to 10 kHz. To digitize it without loss, which sampling rate does the theorem require?
Question 1
Choose an answer

The sampling rate must be greater than twice the highest frequency. Twice 10 kHz is 20 kHz, and exactly 20 kHz is the borderline, so the condition is a rate greater than 20 kHz.

Question 2 Mono audio is sampled at 8 kHz with 8 bits per sample. What is the data rate?
Question 2
Choose an answer

8,000 samples/s × 8 bits × 1 channel = 64,000 bit/s = 64 kbit/s.

Question 3 A 9 Hz sine wave is sampled at 10 Hz. What frequency does the wave through the samples appear to have?
Question 3
Choose an answer

10 Hz is less than twice 9 Hz, so aliasing occurs. The apparent frequency is |9 − 10| = 1 Hz. Set f = 9 and fs = 10 in the chart above to see it.

Question 4 Stereo (2 channels) audio is recorded for one minute at 44.1 kHz and 16 bits per sample. About how large is the data (1 MB = 1,000,000 bytes)?
Question 4
Choose an answer

44,100 × 16 × 2 = 1,411,200 bit/s. × 60 ÷ 8 = 10,584,000 bytes, about 10.6 MB. Watch for mixing up bits and bytes (8 bits).
